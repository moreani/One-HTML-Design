On reading, closely

A stone is the oldest
way to read.

Before spectacles, a reader who could not bring a page into focus had one tool: a dome of polished glass laid flat on the paper.

The stone does not invent light. It recieves what falls on the page and bends it toward the eye, so a line set at six point arrives closer to nine. Letters swell, ink softens, and the words come into focus one at a time.

A good stone is seperate from its stand and warm from the hand. Held to the light it shows a bright ring at the rim and a thin caustic near the base. The first ones occured in the thirteenth century, cut from beryl and set in brass.

On a proof the page slows down. A definately that should read definitely, a comma sitting a line too low, a word that has slipped since the last pull — all of it shows under the glass. Every stone is a small philosphy: reading is not passive, and a page is worth a second pass.
